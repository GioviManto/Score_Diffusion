§1 Why This Experiment?

In a real diffusion model, the key object we need is the score function:

$$s(x, t) = \nabla_x \log p_t(x)$$

This score drives the reverse SDE that generates samples. In practice, we learn it via a neural network because \(p_t(x)\) is intractable. But to build intuition — to understand what the score looks like, how it evolves, and what the geometry of \(p_t\) means — we need a case where everything is known exactly.

The key insight: A Gaussian mixture under OU diffusion stays a Gaussian mixture. Every mean, every covariance, every marginal has a closed-form expression. This is our analytical microscope.

§2 The Setup: Initial Distribution

We choose a 2D Gaussian mixture with \(K = 2\) components:

$$p_0(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{2} w_k \, \mathcal{N}(x;\, \mu_{0,k},\, \Sigma_{0,k})$$

Concretely, the parameters are:

§3 The Forward Process: OU Diffusion

The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) forward SDE gradually destroys structure:

$$dX_t = -X_t \, dt + \sqrt{2} \, dW_t$$

This SDE has an exact solution. If \(X_0\) is any initial point:

$$X_t = e^{-t} X_0 + \sqrt{1 - e^{-2t}} \, Z, \qquad Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

Two competing terms: the signal term \(e^{-t} X_0\) decays exponentially, while the noise term \(\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}\,Z\) grows. At \(t = 0\) we have pure signal; as \(t \to \infty\) we have pure isotropic noise \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\).

Why OU specifically? Its stationary distribution is \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\) — a simple, well-understood noise distribution. And crucially, it maps Gaussian inputs to Gaussian outputs, so Gaussian mixtures stay Gaussian mixtures under this process. That's the analytical tractability we need.

§4 Closed-Form Evolution of Each Component

Because OU maps Gaussians to Gaussians, each component \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0,k}, \Sigma_{0,k})\) evolves as \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_k(t), \Sigma_k(t))\) where:

Mean evolution

$$\mu_k(t) = e^{-t} \mu_{0,k}$$

Exponential decay toward the origin. All structure "forgets" its initial position.

Covariance evolution

$$\Sigma_k(t) = e^{-2t} \Sigma_{0,k} + (1-e^{-2t}) I$$

Anisotropy decays at rate \(e^{-2t}\). Isotropic noise \(I\) fills in as structure disappears.

So the full mixture density at time \(t\) is:

$$p_t(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{2} w_k \, \mathcal{N}\!\left(x;\; \mu_k(t),\; \Sigma_k(t)\right)$$

At \(t \to \infty\): both \(\mu_k(t) \to 0\) and \(\Sigma_k(t) \to I\), so the mixture collapses to \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\) regardless of the initial structure.

§8 Connection to Score-Based Generation

This experiment is an analytical toy model for the theory behind modern diffusion models (DDPM, SMLD, EDM). The key formula connecting forward and reverse processes is Anderson's reverse-time SDE:

$$dX = \bigl[-X + 2\,\nabla_x \log p_t(X)\bigr]\,dt + \sqrt{2}\,d\bar{W}_t$$

where \(d\bar{W}_t\) is a reverse-time Wiener process. The score \(\nabla_x \log p_t\) is the only unknown in the real case. Here, for our Gaussian mixture, it is:

$$\nabla_x \log p_t(x) = \frac{\sum_{k} w_k \, \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_k(t), \Sigma_k(t)) \cdot \Sigma_k(t)^{-1}(\mu_k(t) - x)}{\sum_{k} w_k \, \mathcal{N}(x;\mu_k(t), \Sigma_k(t))}$$

This is an analytically computable, weighted average of component-wise linear scores — a mixture-weighted combination of "pulls" toward each component mean.

Summary of the experiment: We start from a bimodal, correlated Gaussian mixture \(p_0\). We apply OU forward diffusion, which destroys structure exponentially fast. We then play the reverse process and watch structure re-emerge — all in closed form, with no neural network, no approximation. This is the ideal sandbox for understanding what score functions look like, how the geometry of \(p_t\) evolves, and why diffusion models work.
