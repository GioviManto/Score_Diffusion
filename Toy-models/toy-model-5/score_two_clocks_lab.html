Score in Two Clocks:
An Exact Analytical Laboratory

Internal time u drives the system. Diffusion time t corrupts it. Every formula is exact. Every quantity is plotted. No neural networks.

§1 The Two-Clock Architecture

Classical diffusion models live in a single time: the noise level \(t\). Here we distinguish two independent notions of time and study how the score function organises itself across the resulting plane \((u, t)\).

Internal time u

The semantic clock. The system (particle, frame, signal) evolves according to its own Markov dynamics. Think of \(u\) as the frame index in a video: \(u=0,1,2,\ldots\)

Diffusion time t

The noise clock. An independent Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process injects Gaussian noise into any fixed snapshot, corrupting it toward \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) as \(t\to\infty\).

The central object of study is the two-clock score field:

$$s(x,u,t) = \nabla_x \log p_{u,t}(x)$$

where \(p_{u,t}(x)\) is the distribution of the system at internal step \(u\), noised to diffusion level \(t\). We want to understand how this field lives on the plane \((u,t)\) — its norm, geometry, spectrum, and thermodynamic content.

Guiding picture. At each frame \(u\), there are two processes: a forward noising path (black, zig-zag) that carries any state toward \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) as \(t\) grows, and a generative (reverse) path (pink, dashed) driven by the score \(s(x,u,t)\) that reconstructs the frame from noise. Both co-exist at every \(u\).

§2 The Simplest Model: Univariate Gaussian with AR(1) Dynamics

2.1 Initial distribution

We start from the absolutely minimal case: a single univariate Gaussian.

$$x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0,\; \sigma_0^2)$$

2.2 Internal dynamics (AR(1) process)

The simplest Markovian internal evolution is the first-order autoregression: each step applies a contraction \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) and adds i.i.d. noise.

$$x_{u+1} = \alpha\, x_u + \eta_u, \qquad \eta_u \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_\eta^2)$$

Because \(\alpha\lt 1\) the chain is stable. It converges to a stationary distribution as \(u\to\infty\), providing a natural long-time regime to compare against the short-time one.

2.3 Marginal distribution at step u — exact propagation

Since the map is linear and all noise is Gaussian, \(x_u\) is Gaussian for all \(u\). Solving the mean and variance recurrences explicitly:

$$\mu_u = \alpha^u\,\mu_0$$
$$\sigma_u^2 = \alpha^{2u}\,\sigma_0^2 + \sigma_\eta^2\sum_{k=0}^{u-1}\alpha^{2k} = \alpha^{2u}\,\sigma_0^2 + \sigma_\eta^2\,\frac{1-\alpha^{2u}}{1-\alpha^2}$$

Defining the stationary variance \(\sigma_\infty^2 = \sigma_\eta^2/(1-\alpha^2)\), this compresses to a single elegant formula:

$$\boxed{\sigma_u^2 = \sigma_\infty^2 + \alpha^{2u}\!\left(\sigma_0^2 - \sigma_\infty^2\right)}$$
Geometric interpolation. \(\sigma_u^2\) starts at \(\sigma_0^2\) and decays exponentially toward \(\sigma_\infty^2\) at rate \(\alpha^2\) per step. If \(\sigma_0 > \sigma_\infty\) the system focuses; if \(\sigma_0 < \sigma_\infty\) it spreads.

So at each internal step \(u\), the clean distribution is:

$$p_u(x) = \mathcal{N}(x;\;\mu_u,\;\sigma_u^2)$$

§3 Forward Diffusion: The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Kernel

3.1 The OU SDE

$$dX_t = -X_t\,dt + \sqrt{2}\,dW_t, \qquad X_0 = x$$

This SDE has a closed-form solution. Applying Itô's formula or solving directly:

$$X_t \;=\; e^{-t}\,X_0 \;+\; \sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}\;\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

The transition kernel is exactly Gaussian: \(X_t\mid X_0=x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(e^{-t}x_0,\;1-e^{-2t})\). The stationary distribution of this SDE is \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), which is reached as \(t\to\infty\).

3.2 Applying OU to the internal distribution at step u

Given the clean state \(x_u \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_u,\sigma_u^2)\), we apply the OU kernel to obtain the noised observation \(x = e^{-t}x_u + \sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}\,\varepsilon\). Since both \(x_u\) and \(\varepsilon\) are Gaussian, the marginal is again Gaussian:

$$p_{u,t}(x) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x;\;\mu_u(t),\;\sigma_u(t)^2\right)$$

with exact parameters:

$$\mu_u(t) = e^{-t}\,\mu_u = e^{-t}\,\alpha^u\,\mu_0$$
$$\sigma_u(t)^2 = e^{-2t}\,\sigma_u^2 + \left(1-e^{-2t}\right) = e^{-2t}\!\left[\sigma_\infty^2 + \alpha^{2u}(\sigma_0^2 - \sigma_\infty^2)\right] + 1 - e^{-2t}$$
$$\boxed{\sigma_u(t)^2 = 1 + e^{-2t}\left(\sigma_u^2 - 1\right)}$$
Three competing scales. The variance \(\sigma_u(t)^2\) interpolates between \(\sigma_u^2\) (at \(t=0\)) and \(1\) (at \(t\to\infty\)). Meanwhile the mean \(\mu_u(t)=e^{-t}\alpha^u\mu_0\) decays doubly exponentially — once from internal dynamics, once from diffusion.

§4 The Score Function: Exact Derivation

4.1 From log-density to score

For \(p_{u,t}(x) = \mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma^2)\), the log-density is:

$$\log p_{u,t}(x) = -\frac{(x-\mu_u(t))^2}{2\,\sigma_u(t)^2} - \frac{1}{2}\log\!\left(2\pi\,\sigma_u(t)^2\right)$$

Taking the gradient with respect to \(x\):

$$\boxed{s(x,u,t) = \nabla_x\log p_{u,t}(x) = -\frac{x - \mu_u(t)}{\sigma_u(t)^2}}$$

This is the exact, closed-form score. It is a linear function of \(x\): a restoring force pointing toward the mean \(\mu_u(t)\), with stiffness \(1/\sigma_u(t)^2\).

4.2 Score as gradient of free energy

Define the free energy landscape (negative log-density):

$$F(x,u,t) := -\log p_{u,t}(x) = \frac{(x-\mu_u(t))^2}{2\,\sigma_u(t)^2} + \frac{1}{2}\log\!\left(2\pi\,\sigma_u(t)^2\right)$$

Then the score is exactly the downhill gradient of this landscape:

$$s(x,u,t) = -\nabla_x F(x,u,t)$$
Thermodynamic analogy. \(F(x,u,t)\) is a parabolic potential (harmonic oscillator!). The score-driven reverse diffusion is literally a gradient flow descending this bowl. As \(t\) decreases, the bowl narrows (more focused minimum) and the mean shifts toward the true data mean \(\mu_u\). Generation = climbing out of the flat standard-Gaussian bowl and sliding into the increasingly sharp data bowl.

4.3 Score norm

The norm of the score field, evaluated at displacement \(d = x - \mu_u(t)\) from the mean:

$$\|s(x,u,t)\| = \frac{|x - \mu_u(t)|}{\sigma_u(t)^2}$$

At the mean: score is zero. The score grows linearly with distance and shrinks with variance.

§5 Jacobian, Spectrum, and the 2D Extension

5.1 Score Jacobian in 1D

The Jacobian of the score with respect to \(x\) is simply:

$$J_s(u,t) = \frac{\partial s}{\partial x}(x,u,t) = -\frac{1}{\sigma_u(t)^2}$$

Crucially, this is constant in \(x\) (the Gaussian score is linear, its derivative is flat). The single eigenvalue is \(\lambda = -1/\sigma_u(t)^2\), always negative — a globally stable restoring field.

5.2 Multivariate extension: 2D Gaussian with anisotropic covariance

Let \(x\in\mathbb{R}^2\), initial distribution \(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu_0, \Sigma_0)\) with \(\Sigma_0 = \begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{x}^2 & \rho\sigma_x\sigma_y\\\rho\sigma_x\sigma_y&\sigma_y^2\end{pmatrix}\).

We use a rotating AR(1) dynamics for the internal time:

$$\boldsymbol a(u+1) = \alpha\,R_{\Delta\theta}\,\boldsymbol a(u) + \boldsymbol\xi_u, \qquad \boldsymbol\xi_u\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf 0,\,\sigma_\eta^2 I_2)$$ $$R_{\Delta\theta} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\Delta\theta & -\sin\Delta\theta\\\sin\Delta\theta & \cos\Delta\theta\end{pmatrix}$$

Since \(R_{\Delta\theta}\) is orthogonal (\(RR^\top = I\)), the covariance propagates as:

$$\Sigma_{u+1} = \alpha^2\,R_{\Delta\theta}\,\Sigma_u\,R_{\Delta\theta}^\top + \sigma_\eta^2 I_2$$

Let \(\Sigma_0 = V\,\text{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2)\,V^\top\) be the eigendecomposition. After \(u\) steps, the eigenvectors rotate by \(u\Delta\theta\) and the eigenvalues evolve as:

$$\lambda_k(u) = \sigma_\infty^2 + \alpha^{2u}\!\left(\lambda_k - \sigma_\infty^2\right), \qquad \sigma_\infty^2 = \frac{\sigma_\eta^2}{1-\alpha^2}$$

So \(\Sigma_u = (R_{u\Delta\theta} V)\,\text{diag}(\lambda_1(u),\lambda_2(u))\,(R_{u\Delta\theta} V)^\top\).

After OU diffusion at time \(t\):

$$\Sigma_u(t) = e^{-2t}\,\Sigma_u + (1-e^{-2t})\,I_2$$

Eigenvalues of \(\Sigma_u(t)\):

$$\lambda_k(u,t) = e^{-2t}\,\lambda_k(u) + 1 - e^{-2t} = 1 + e^{-2t}\left(\lambda_k(u) - 1\right)$$

The score field and its Jacobian are:

$$s(\mathbf x,u,t) = -\Sigma_u(t)^{-1}\!\left(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu_u(t)\right)$$ $$J_s(u,t) = -\Sigma_u(t)^{-1}$$

Jacobian eigenvalues:

$$\lambda_k^J(u,t) = -\frac{1}{\lambda_k(u,t)}$$
Dimensional collapse. As \(t\to\infty\): \(\lambda_k(u,t)\to 1\) so all \(\lambda_k^J\to -1\). The two Jacobian eigenvalues merge — all directions become equally stiff. This is "dimensional collapse": the anisotropy of the data distribution becomes invisible at large noise levels. The score cannot distinguish directions tangent to the data from perpendicular ones.

§6 Entropy, Fisher Information, and Free Energy

6.1 Differential entropy

For \(p_{u,t} = \mathcal{N}(\mu_u(t),\sigma_u(t)^2)\), the differential entropy is:

$$H(u,t) = \frac{1}{2}\log\!\left(2\pi e\,\sigma_u(t)^2\right)$$

Entropy depends only on the variance, not the mean. Since \(\sigma_u(t)^2\) increases with \(t\) (adding noise) and converges with \(u\) (toward stationary), the entropy landscape on the \((u,t)\) plane is a smooth monotone surface.

6.2 Fisher information

The Fisher information (expected squared score) is:

$$\mathcal{I}(u,t) = \mathbb{E}_{p_{u,t}}\!\left[\|s(x,u,t)\|^2\right] = \frac{1}{\sigma_u(t)^2}$$

It is the reciprocal of the variance, and the reciprocal of \(-J_s(u,t)\). This is the Cramér–Rao bound in disguise: small variance (sharp distribution) \(\Leftrightarrow\) large Fisher information \(\Leftrightarrow\) stiff Jacobian.

Triad identity for Gaussians: $$\mathcal{I}(u,t) = -J_s(u,t) = \frac{1}{\sigma_u(t)^2} = \frac{1}{1+e^{-2t}(\sigma_u^2-1)}$$ Fisher information, Jacobian magnitude, and inverse variance are all the same object.

6.3 Free energy surface

The expected free energy (KL divergence to standard normal, a.k.a. negative ELBO) is:

$$\langle F\rangle(u,t) = \mathbb{E}_{p_{u,t}}[-\log p_{u,t}(x)] = H(u,t) = \frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e\,\sigma_u(t)^2)$$

The score-driven flow is gradient descent on \(F(x,u,t)\). At fixed \((u,t)\), the flow is exactly that of an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck potential centered at \(\mu_u(t)\).

§7 The \((u,t)\) Plane: Regimes

Fix a test point \(x\). The score norm \(|s(x,u,t)|\) defines a landscape on the plane \((u,t)\in[0,U]\times[0,T]\). Four qualitatively distinct regimes arise:

I — Sharp signal (small t, small u) II — Best window (intermediate t, u) III — Stationary (large u) IV — Universal noise (large t)

Phase I — Sharp signal

Small \(t\), small \(u\). Distribution is close to \(p_0\). Score reflects original anisotropy and mean. Fisher information is high. Jacobian eigenvalues are spread apart. Best place to read data geometry.

Phase IV — Universal noise

Large \(t\). Regardless of \(u\), the distribution \(p_{u,t}\approx\mathcal{N}(0,1)\). Score \(\approx -x\). Jacobian \(\approx -1\). All internal-time information erased.

Phase II — Intermediate window

Intermediate \(t\). Score geometry is rich: eigenvalue gaps are opened, anisotropy is visible, but the distribution is not too sharp. Best window to study a propagator \(P\) across \(u\) (cf. Achilli §4).

Phase III — Stationary regime

Large \(u\). Distribution converges to stationary \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\infty^2)\). Score becomes independent of further increases in \(u\). Apparent stationarity — but could be trivial (memorisation).

Two-time autocorrelation. Fix a point \(x_0\) and consider the score value \(s(x_0,u,t)\) as a function of \(u\) for fixed \(t\). This is a time series driven by the AR(1) dynamics. Its autocorrelation decays as \(\alpha^{|u'-u|}\) (in mean) and \(\alpha^{2|u'-u|}\) (in variance). The score inherits the memory of the internal dynamics.

§8 What Lies Beyond Gaussians

The Gaussian is the unique distribution for which the score is linear in \(x\). The first genuinely new phenomenon appears with a mixture:

$$p_u(x) = \sum_{k=1}^K w_k\,\mathcal{N}(x;\mu_{u,k},\sigma_{u,k}^2)$$

The score becomes a nonlinear weighted average of component-wise linear scores:

$$s(x,u,t) = \sum_k r_k(x,u,t)\cdot s_k(x,u,t), \qquad r_k = \frac{w_k\,\phi_k(x)}{\sum_j w_j\,\phi_j(x)}$$

The responsibilities \(r_k(x,u,t)\) introduce genuine nonlinearity and multi-scale structure. The score is no longer constant in its Jacobian. Eigenvalue gaps are no longer trivially determined by covariance matrices alone. This is the first level where the spectral structure becomes non-trivial — and the first level where Achilli's memorisation / collapse analysis becomes interesting.
