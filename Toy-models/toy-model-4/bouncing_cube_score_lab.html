Bouncing particle in a cube (drift + Brownian noise) — marginal vs conditional score

You’ll see the motion as an animation, and (below) two vector fields: the marginal score on a slice, and the conditional score given the recent past.

1 Setup: a noisy billiard in a cube

We model an “electron” moving inside a perfectly reflecting cube \([0,L]^3\). Each discrete internal-time step \(u \mapsto u+1\) combines: (i) a deterministic drift (momentum-like) and (ii) an isotropic Gaussian perturbation. When the particle hits a wall, it reflects specularly (the normal component of velocity flips sign, magnitude preserved).

\[ \textbf{Unconstrained proposal:}\qquad y_{u+1} = x_u + v_u\,\Delta u + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta u}\,\xi_u,\qquad \xi_u\sim\mathcal N(0, I_3). \] \[ \textbf{Reflection:}\qquad x_{u+1} = \mathrm{Fold}(y_{u+1})\in[0,L]^3,\qquad v_{u+1}=\mathrm{ReflectVel}(v_u, y_{u+1}). \]

The Fold map is a coordinate-wise “mirror folding” with period \(2L\): it takes any real \(y\) and maps it into \([0,L]\) by reflecting every other length-\(L\) interval. This produces the same geometry you’d get from perfect elastic bounces.

Why this is nice for scores: the one-step conditional density \(p(x_{u+1}\mid x_u, v_u)\) is the density of a folded Gaussian. That gives a clean expression for the conditional score \(\nabla_{x_{u+1}}\log p(x_{u+1}\mid x_u, v_u)\).

2 What we plot: two score fields

We care about the score as a vector field: \(s(x,u) = \nabla_x \log p_u(x)\). Here there are two different (but related) objects:

Marginal score (left plot below): \(s_{\text{marg}}(x,u)=\nabla_x\log p_u(x)\), where \(p_u\) is the distribution of positions after \(u\) bounces. We estimate \(p_u\) from many simulated particles (Monte Carlo) using a Gaussian KDE; then the KDE score has a closed form.
Conditional score (right plot below): \(s_{\text{cond}}(x,u)=\nabla_{x_{u+1}}\log p(x_{u+1}=x\mid \text{past})\). If the state is \((x_u,v_u)\), the dynamics is Markov and conditioning on the entire past reduces to conditioning on \((x_u,v_u)\). If you only observe positions, you can approximate momentum from \((x_{u}-x_{u-1})/\Delta u\) (toggle below).

Folded-Gaussian transition density (1D building block)

\[ \text{Let } m = x_u + v_u\Delta u,\quad s^2=\sigma^2\Delta u.\quad \text{For } x\in[0,L], \] \[ p_{\mathrm{1D}}(x\mid m) = \sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}\Big[\varphi(x+2kL;\,m,s^2)+\varphi(-x+2kL;\,m,s^2)\Big], \] where \(\varphi(\cdot; m, s^2)\) is the Gaussian density. For the cube with independent coordinates, \(p(x\mid x_u,v_u)=\prod_{i=1}^3 p_{\mathrm{1D}}(x_i\mid m_i)\) and the score components add in log-space.

3 Sources (what this page is building on)

The key mathematical ideas behind the conditional kernel are: (i) specular reflection (flip normal component of velocity) and (ii) constructing reflected kernels by folding or “method of images”. The folded normal distribution is the simplest one-wall case (“fold a Gaussian at 0”). A short, clean reference for the interval heat kernels (Dirichlet/Neumann) and their geometric folding interpretation is included below.

  • Heat kernels on intervals; reflected (Neumann) case and folding construction: Teplyaev (notes).
  • Reflected Brownian motion overview + intuition for reflection at a barrier: Varadhan (NYU notes).
  • Folded normal distribution (density as sum of two Gaussians, the 1-wall image idea): Wikipedia.
  • Specular reflection physics intuition (perfect mirror reflection preserves magnitude): PBRT v4 (Specular reflection).

3 What to look for (intuition)

  • Near walls, density piles up anisotropically because reflection maps out-of-box proposals back inside; the score points away from low-probability regions (often “into” the box).
  • Increasing σ makes the marginal density flatter; scores shrink in magnitude (roughly like \(1/h\) for KDE and like \(1/\sigma^2\Delta u\) for the one-step Gaussian core).
  • Conditional score should roughly point from your current mean drift \(m=x_u+v\Delta u\) toward the likely post-bounce locations (multi-image contributions matter when \(\sigma\sqrt{\Delta u}\) is not tiny).
  • If you switch Conditional score uses to infer v, you’ll see how “conditioning on positions only” can mis-estimate momentum right after a bounce.
